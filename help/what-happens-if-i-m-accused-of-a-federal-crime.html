What Happens If I'm Accused of a Federal Crime?

Home About Services Contact FAQs Articles Case Studies Attorneys Web Pages

Step 1: Investigation and Arrest — This phase often begins long before you even know you're under scrutiny. Federal agencies like the FBI, DEA, or IRS conduct thorough investigations, which can involve surveillance, interviews, and grand jury subpoenas. An arrest might come after an indictment or a complaint, and you'll typically be taken into custody by federal marshals. This can last anywhere from weeks to months, depending on the complexity of the case, and it’s a critical time to have legal counsel.

Step 2: Initial Appearance and Bail Hearing — Soon after your arrest, usually within 24-48 hours, you'll have an initial appearance before a federal magistrate judge. The judge will inform you of the charges, your rights, and determine if you can be released on bail. The court considers factors like flight risk and danger to the community. Having an attorney at this stage is crucial to argue for your release on favorable terms.

Step 3: Grand Jury Indictment (or Information) — For many federal felonies, a grand jury must formally indict you. This secret proceeding involves prosecutors presenting evidence to a group of citizens who decide if there’s probable cause to believe a crime was committed and you committed it. If they find sufficient evidence, they issue an indictment. If no indictment, the case might proceed by information, typically when a plea agreement is in the works. This can take weeks.

Step 4: Arraignment and Pre-Trial Motions — After indictment, you’ll be arraigned, where you officially enter a plea (guilty, not guilty, or nolo contendere). Following this, your attorney will file pre-trial motions to challenge evidence, suppress statements, or request discovery from the prosecution. These motions are key to shaping the case and can last several months.

Step 5: Plea Bargaining or Trial — Many federal cases are resolved through plea agreements, where you plead guilty to lesser charges or in exchange for a lighter sentence. If no plea is reached, the case goes to trial, where a jury or judge hears evidence and decides your guilt or innocence. Federal trials can be lengthy and very complex, often lasting weeks. Expert legal representation is essential for either path.

Step 6: Sentencing (if convicted) — If you plead guilty or are found guilty at trial, the judge will determine your sentence. This involves complex federal sentencing guidelines, which are advisory but heavily influence the outcome. Your attorney will advocate for the most lenient sentence possible, presenting mitigating factors. This usually occurs a few months after conviction to allow for a pre-sentence investigation report.
