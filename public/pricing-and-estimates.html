Getting into a car accident is stressful enough, but making certain missteps afterward can seriously jeopardize your personal injury claim. You want to protect your ability to recover fair compensation, so steer clear of these common mistakes.

1. Don't delay seeking medical attention: Even if you feel fine right after the crash, adrenaline can mask injuries. Many serious issues, like whiplash or concussions, can take days to manifest. Go to a doctor right away to get checked out. Delaying can make it seem like your injuries aren't accident-related.

2. Don't give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurance company: They're not looking out for you. Any statement you give can be twisted or used to minimize what they owe. Politely decline and tell them your attorney will be in touch.

3. Don't accept a quick settlement offer: Early offers are almost always lowball attempts to close your claim before you even know the full extent of your injuries and losses. Once you accept, you usually can't ask for more.

4. Don't fail to collect evidence at the scene: If you can safely do so, get photos, videos, witness contact info, and the other driver's details. The more evidence you have, the stronger your case will be.

5. Don't admit fault or apologize: Even a casual 'I'm sorry' can be misinterpreted as an admission of guilt, even if you weren't at fault. Stick to the facts when talking to police and other drivers.

6. Don't neglect your doctor's orders: If you don't follow through with prescribed treatments, physical therapy, or follow-up appointments, the insurance company might argue that your injuries aren't as severe as you claim or that you hindered your own recovery. Show up to everything and keep records.

Having a criminal conviction on your record can feel like a heavy burden, impacting everything from job applications and housing to professional licenses and even your peace of mind. It’s a common misconception that once you're convicted in a state court, that's just how it is forever. But here in California, that's often not the case! The state actually has some of the most expansive post-conviction relief laws around, offering real pathways to move forward.

Here are some myths and facts about clearing your record in California

Myth: An expungement completely erases my criminal record. Fact: While an expungement (officially called a 'dismissal' under Penal Code 1203.4) is incredibly helpful, it doesn't literally erase your record. It changes your plea from guilty/no contest to not guilty, and the case is dismissed. This means you can often truthfully state you haven't been convicted for most purposes (like private employment). However, for certain licensing boards or future criminal charges, the conviction might still be considered. It significantly improves your prospects, though!

Myth: I can't get my felony reduced to a misdemeanor. Fact: Depending on the original offense and how your sentence was served, many felonies can indeed be reduced to misdemeanors under California law. This is a huge benefit, as misdemeanors carry far fewer restrictions and social stigmas than felonies. Angelo can evaluate your situation to see if this option applies to you.

Myth: There's only one way to clear a criminal record. Fact: California offers several paths for post-conviction relief beyond just expungement. These include Certificates of Rehabilitation, Governor's Pardons, and sealing arrest records. The best option for you depends on the specific facts of your case, the type of conviction, and your current circumstances. An attorney can help determine the most effective strategy.

Myth: It's too late to do anything about an old conviction. Fact: Many post-conviction relief options don't have a strict time limit. Even convictions from years ago can often be addressed, especially as laws change and evolve. It's never too late to explore your options and see if you're eligible for relief. A past conviction doesn't have to define your entire future.

Myth: I can handle the expungement process myself. Fact: While theoretically possible, the process involves complex legal filings, understanding specific penal codes, and potentially attending court hearings. Errors can lead to delays or outright denial.
