Misdemeanor Charge in San Diego: Myths vs. Facts

Home About Services Contact FAQs Articles Case Studies Attorneys Web Pages

When you’re facing a misdemeanor charge in San Diego, it’s easy to get caught up in rumors or misunderstandings about what’s actually happening. Let’s clear up some common myths with solid facts, so you can make informed decisions. Attorney Angelo J. Reyes helps clients across San Diego County understand their real options and navigate these situations.

Myth: A misdemeanor isn't a big deal and won't really affect my life.

Fact: While not as severe as a felony, a misdemeanor conviction can definitely have lasting consequences. It can show up on background checks, impact your employment opportunities, housing applications, and even professional licenses. Depending on the charge, there could be fines, probation, and even jail time. Ignoring it is never a good idea.

Myth: I should talk to the police and explain my side of the story; it’ll help them understand.

Fact: This is a huge misconception. Anything you say to law enforcement, even if you think it's an explanation, can be used against you. You have the right to remain silent, and it’s always best to exercise that right and politely state that you want to speak with an attorney before answering any questions.

Myth: I can just represent myself in court for a misdemeanor; it’s simple enough.

Fact: The legal system is incredibly complex, even for misdemeanor cases. There are specific rules of evidence, court procedures, and legal arguments that need to be followed. Without a deep understanding, you could inadvertently harm your own defense. An experienced attorney knows how to navigate these complexities and protect your rights effectively.

Myth: If the police didn’t read me my Miranda rights, my case will be dismissed.

Fact: Miranda rights only apply when you are in police custody AND being interrogated. If you weren't being questioned while detained, or if you weren't in custody, then Miranda warnings aren't necessarily required. Even if they were violated, it doesn't automatically mean your case is dismissed; it usually means certain statements might be excluded from evidence. This is a complex area where legal counsel is essential.
